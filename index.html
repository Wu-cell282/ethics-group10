Building a Sustainable 2030

The 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, adopted by all United Nations Member States in 2015, provides a shared blueprint for peace and prosperity for people and the planet (UN, 2015).

Why UNs SDGs Guidance Matters

The world faces interconnected challenges ranging from climate change to systemic inequality. The SDGs act as a universal framework to address these "wicked problems" simultaneously. By providing clear targets, the guidance ensures that development in one area does not compromise sustainability in another, fostering a global partnership that leaves no one behind (Global Goals, 2023).

Selected Goal

SDG 11: Sustainable Cities & Communities

The Purpose
of SDG 11

Sustainable Development Goal 11 aims to make cities and human settlements inclusive, safe, resilient and sustainable. It directly focuses on disaster risk reduction, sustainable transport, and creating safe human settlements.

Target Country

Kenya

Currently facing critical unsustainable urban development challenges, characterized by rapid demographic growth and infrastructure lag.

Adverse Impact Analysis

Kenya suffers from serious unsustainable urban development problems. In major cities, large-scale slums are constantly expanding, trapped in overcrowding, dilapidated housing, and highly insufficient living space. This phenomenon of informal slum settlements has become extremely common across urban areas.

60%+
of the urban residents live in poor and informal communities, where excessive population density, poor housing, and lack of basic services are widespread. (Source: KNBS, 2019, p.45)
The public transportation system is lagging behind, leading to widespread and severe traffic congestion. Traffic Lag
Statistical Reference
Figure 1: Levels & Rates of Urbanisation (1948-2035)

Source: 2019 KPHC Analytical Report Vol. IX; World Cities Report, 2022

Root Causes

01. Rural-to-Urban Migration Limited rural employment opportunities have led to a massive migration of people to Kenya's cities. The large influx of migrants puts pressure on urban systems, while local authorities, with limited funding and planning capacity, struggle to cope with population growth.

— UNDP, 2023

02. Velocity Imbalance The growth of urban population far outpaces the development speed of housing, infrastructure and public services. Regional economic inequality has driven more low-income groups to flock to cities, resulting in a severe imbalance between supply and demand.

— NCPD, 2025

03. Capacity & Regulation Deficit Urban planning and land management have failed to keep pace with the rapid population growth. Inadequate supervision has led to the emergence of numerous disorderly residential areas and the uncontrolled expansion of informal housing.

— UNDP, 2023

04. Disorderly Layout The lack of unified planning in urban layout has led to the disorderly presence of illegal buildings; insufficient investment in infrastructure fails to meet the needs of residents, resulting in slums, chaotic traffic and poor urban living conditions.

— UNDP, 2023
Improvement Measures & Strategy
Measure A
Scientific Planning & In-situ Upgrades

Formulate a scientific and unified overall urban planning to renovate chaotic slums and build affordable, compliant housing. Emphasize no forced demolition; existing structures will be preserved while focusing on low-cost essential upgrades to surrounding infrastructure.

— World Bank, 2016
Measure B
Diversified External Funding

To counter local funding shortages, avoid relying solely on government finance. Actively absorb international public welfare funds, overseas development assistance (ODA), and foreign capital to expand the public transport network and relieve traffic pressure.

— UN-Habitat, 2018
Measure C
Environmental Management & Resilience

Establish a standardized system for waste classification and disposal to strengthen environmental hygiene and maintain cleanliness. Concurrently, upgrade urban disaster prevention and emergency infrastructure to enhance the city's coping capacity against natural disasters.

Measure D
Coordinated Rural-Urban Development

Coordinate urban-rural development patterns to alleviate the current stress of excessive populations flocking to big cities. Actively promote balanced economic growth and infrastructure construction in small towns.

SDG 14: Life Below Water

The Purpose
of SDG 14

Sustainable Development Goal 14 aims to conserve and sustainably use the oceans, seas, and marine resources for sustainable development.

Moreover, it directly addresses marine pollution, overfishing, ocean acidification, protection of marine and coastal ecosystems, and support for small-scale fishers.

Target Country

The Philippines

Home to one of the world’s largest coral reef systems (approximately 27,000 km²), currently facing severe marine degradation and critical ecological pressure.

Adverse Impact Analysis

The Philippines suffers from critical threats to its coastal and marine environment. Coral reefs across the archipelago have experienced significant decline, leaving many surveyed areas in poor to fair condition. Overfishing, destructive fishing practices, and coastal habitat loss further jeopardize marine biodiversity and the livelihoods of millions dependent on fisheries and tourism.

356,371 MT
of ocean plastic pollution emitted annually. The country currently ranks as the world’s top contributor to land-based plastic waste entering marine ecosystems.
Destructive fishing practices and habitat loss are driving an urgent biodiversity crisis across major fishing grounds. Biodiversity Threat
Statistical Reference
Figure 1: Trends in Live Coral Cover in the Philippines (1980-2030 Projection)

Source: Global Coral Reef Monitoring Network (GCRMN), 2020

Root Causes

01. Infrastructure Pressures Rapid population growth, intense urbanization, and severely lagging waste management infrastructure have led to massive land-based plastic pollution entering rivers and oceans, especially during peak typhoon seasons.

— Oruc Baci et al., 2026

02. Poverty Causes Destruction Widespread coastal poverty paired with a high commercial demand for seafood drives overfishing and illegal practices like blast and cyanide fishing, which instantly shatter live coral structures.

— GCRMN, 2020

03. Anthropogenic Climate Stressors Global climate change-induced sea temperature spikes and worsening ocean acidification trigger widespread, repeated coral bleaching events, heavily diminishing regional live coral cover.

— GCRMN, 2020

04. Governance & Regulation Deficit Weak enforcement of environmental laws, insufficient urban municipal planning, and destructive land-based activities (agricultural runoff, mining, and chaotic coastal development) result in extensive sedimentation and eutrophication.

— Dagum et al., 2024
Improvement Measures & Strategy
Measure A
Waste Management Upgrades

Strengthen national and local waste management systems by implementing strict “zero-sachet” policies, expanding community-based recycling initiatives, and investing heavily in modern waste treatment facilities within urban river catchments. A synchronized combination of infrastructure and behavioral change is essential.

Measure B
Fisheries Enforcement & MPAs

Enhance enforcement of the regional Fisheries Code and scale up well-managed Marine Protected Areas (MPAs). Deploy advanced satellite monitoring and localized community patrols to systematically eliminate illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) fishing. Mobilize international development assistance and blue bonds to fund operations.

Measure C
Ecosystem Restoration & Resilient Strains

Launch large-scale coral reef restoration projects utilizing dedicated ocean nurseries and climate-resilient coral strains. Concurrently restore surrounding mangrove forests and seagrass beds to construct natural, high-resilience coastal protection barriers.

Measure D
Alternative Livelihoods & Stewardship

Promote sustainable alternative livelihoods such as coastal ecotourism, seaweed farming, and certified sustainable fisheries to relieve wild stocks. Integrate ocean education into school curricula, strengthen inter-agency coordination, and foster public-private-civil society partnerships to secure long-term marine stewardship.

SDG 13: Climate Action

The Purpose
of SDG 13

Sustainable Development Goal 13 demands urgent, synchronized global action to combat climate change and its far-reaching ecological and socioeconomic impacts.

It strictly focuses on strengthening adaptive capacity, integrating climate measures into national policy, and improving education and institutional awareness.

Target Country

Tuvalu

A low-lying Pacific reef atoll nation structurally highly vulnerable to rapid sea-level rise, ocean warming, and violent weather anomalies.

Adverse Impact Analysis

Tuvalu faces catastrophic existential threats driven by global anthropogenic emissions. The compounding effects of rising sea levels, high-velocity tropical cyclones, and extensive king tides have led to accelerated shoreline erosion and systemic saltwater intrusion into arable lands. This process rapidly contaminates critical underground freshwater lenses and shatters local subsistence agriculture.

5 mm/yr
of localized sea-level rise—significantly higher than the global average—directly triggering severe land loss and forced demographic displacement risks.
Widespread marine inundation and acute soil salinization are crippling food security and forcing urban overcrowding. Inundation Lag
Statistical Reference
Figure 1: Observed Sea-Level Rise at Funafuti, Tuvalu (1992–2020)

Source: University of Colorado CCAR Altimetry Project; UHSLC Tide Gauge Data, 2020; SPREP (2024)

Root Causes

01. Global Carbon Emission Unchecked industrial greenhouse gas emissions from major global economies accelerate global warming and thermal expansion of small island oceans, forcing localized sea levels upward.

— IPCC, 2021

02. Topographical Vulnerability The average elevation of Tuvalu’s atolls is less than 2-3 meters above sea level. This extreme structural plateau offers no natural geological barriers against high swells or ocean surges.

— SPREP, 2024

03. Thermal Inertia & Coral Decay Elevated ocean temperature anomalies trigger extensive marine bleaching, which systematically degrades the surrounding protective coral reefs, removing the island's primary shield against ocean wave energy.

— NOAA, 2025

04. Local Adaptation & Capital Deficit The municipal planning sectors suffer from severe domestic funding constraints, a lack of unified global climate finance distribution, and insufficient technological support to execute mega-scale land reclamation.

— UNDP, 2023
Improvement Measures & Strategy
Measure A
Coastal Reclamation & Hard Engineering

Formulate a standardized, long-term engineering blueprint to build elevated sea walls and expand coastal land reclamation using dredged sand. Emphasize nature-based defenses; gray concrete structures must integrate with dense coastal vegetation to prevent sand erosion.

Measure B
International Climate Sovereignty & Finance

To counter internal funding shortages, optimize international climate litigation and systematically absorb resources from the UN Green Climate Fund (GCF) and Overseas Development Assistance (ODA) to reinforce infrastructure resilience.

Measure C
Freshwater Desalination & Biosafety

Establish localized, community-scale solar desalination networks to counter saltwater intrusion. Simultaneously expand advanced rainwater collection facilities and introduce salt-tolerant crop strains to stabilize agricultural health.

Measure D
Digital Nation Sovereignty & Contingency

Coordinate regional relocation protocols and pioneer digital administrative mirroring projects (e.g., storing cultural identity in cloud servers) to safeguard legal statehood sovereignty against severe sea-level risks.

Field Photography: Tuvalu Atoll Vulnerability

Visual documentation of shoreline erosion, rising tides, and community resilience mechanisms.

SDG 7: Affordable & Clean Energy

The Purpose
of SDG 7

Sustainable Development Goal 7 aims to ensure access to affordable, reliable, sustainable, and modern energy for all.

For South Africa, this goal is not just about environmental protection, but a critical foundation for national economic survival and social equity.

Target Country

South Africa

Currently facing an acute, structural electricity supply crisis, tipping the national grid between cascading blackouts and urgent system modernization.

Adverse Impacts & Quantitative Analysis

South Africa's energy landscape has reached a critical tipping point. The nation's heavy reliance on an aging coal fleet has led to an exponential increase in destructive power outages, locally known as load shedding. These continuous blackouts have crippled the manufacturing and mining sectors, while frequent outages disrupt hospital operations and compromise public safety at night, disproportionately affecting vulnerable communities in informal settlements.

6,837 Hrs
Total duration of load shedding reached in 2023—nearly 78% of the entire year under power restrictions.
190%+
Electricity tariff hike in the last decade, causing widespread energy poverty and failing affordability.
According to the South African Reserve Bank, ongoing load shedding shaves off up to 2 percentage points from national GDP growth annually. GDP Drag
Statistical Reference
Figure 2: South Africa's Energy Fleet Performance & Generation Shares

Source: CSIR Energy Centre & Stats SA (2025/2026)

Root Causes

01. Infrastructure Decay The Energy Availability Factor (EAF) of Eskom's coal fleet has plummeted from 70% in 2019 to below 55% in 2023, primarily driven by systemic lack of maintenance and embedded corruption.

02. Market Monopoly Deficit For decades, a single state-owned utility monopoly (Eskom) controlled the power landscape, heavily discouraging private investment in renewables and creating a vulnerable single point of failure.

— Eskom Market Review, 2025

03. The Severe "Coal Trap" Despite global decarbonization pressures, South Africa's vast domestic coal reserves—which still account for roughly 80% of total generation—have created an intensely rigid path-dependency.

04. Political & Labor Complexity Transitioning rapidly away from the traditional fossil framework directly threatens thousands of essential mining jobs, making the execution of a "Just Energy Transition" highly complex.

Personal Response & Strategic Improvement Measures

To move from "Adversity" to "Prosperity," South Africa must pivot from centralized coal to decentralized renewables:

Measure A
Grid Modernization & Transmission Upgrades

The government must prioritize aggressively upgrading the physical transmission grid. Currently, many wind and solar projects in the clean energy-rich Northern Cape cannot connect to the national grid because the existing wires are operating at full capacity.

Measure B
Empowering the Private Sector & Feed-in Tariffs

Capitalizing on the trend where renewable share successfully rose to 16.2%, the state should continuously remove restrictive licensing caps for private generators, allowing commercial businesses to seamlessly sell excess solar power back to the grid via optimized Feed-in Tariffs.

Measure C
Targeted Social Subsidies (FBE Expansion)

To firmly guarantee that "No one is left behind," the Free Basic Electricity (FBE) quota must be systematically increased for registered indigent households to directly mitigate the severe socioeconomic impacts of rising utility tariffs.

Measure D
The JET-IP Labor Retraining Framework

South Africa must transparently and efficiently utilize the $8.5 billion Just Energy Transition Partnership (JETP) funds to systematically retrain legacy coal miners for technical jobs within the emerging wind and solar industries, ensuring a socially fair transition.

Visual documentation of urban load shedding impacts, grid maintenance constraints, and the rapid deployment of rural and commercial solar structures.

Group Understanding: Carbon Footprint

The understanding of Carbon Footprint Carbon footprint refers to the total amount of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases emitted by humans, organizations or activities. It includes both direct emissions in daily life and indirect emissions during production, transportation and consumption processes. Carbon footprint is used to measure the extent to which human activities contribute to global warming.

The objective of “Reduce, reuse, repair, recycle”

Theory Framework
The objective of “Save energy at home”

The primary objective of “Save energy at home” is to optimize residential energy efficiency and cultivate a sustainable consumption ethos. This action recognizes that domestic electricity demand is a significant driver of global carbon emissions, particularly in regions still dependent on fossil fuel power generation. By implementing strategic energy-saving measures, individuals can reduce their "energy intensity"—the amount of energy required to support their daily lifestyle—thereby mitigating the environmental degradation associated with resource extraction and greenhouse gas emissions.

Beyond immediate carbon reduction, this action aims to foster "energy literacy," empowering individuals to understand the systemic link between personal habits and global climate stability. In a world facing both energy security challenges and climate emergencies, saving energy at home serves as a practical, scalable, and high-impact contribution to the transition toward a low-carbon society. It aligns with the principles of responsible consumption and serves as a critical personal intervention.

‘Carbon Offset’ Solution 01
Phantom Load Management

Eliminating structural standby "phantom load" across household appliances reduces total electricity waste.

Ref: Shuma et al. (2022)
‘Carbon Offset’ Solution 02
LED Technology Transition

Replacing traditional incandescent bulbs with Light Emitting Diode (LED) technology is a high-impact technical intervention. LEDs consume up to 85% less energy and have a significantly longer lifespan compared to conventional lighting. This transition reduces the frequency of replacements and decreases the demand on the power grid. By optimizing my living space with LED technology, I am contributing to a cumulative reduction in $CO_2$ emissions and promoting a more sustainable approach to residential lighting (Santamouris & Paolini, 2023).

Ref: Santamouris & Paolini (2023)
‘Carbon Offset’ Solution 03
Strategic management of high-power appliances

Adopting eco-settings on intensive equipment and utilizing natural solar illumination alongside blind thermal regulation diminishes mechanical reliance.

Ref: (United Nations, n.d.)
